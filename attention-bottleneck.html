01 Premise

The observation

The work that's left is made only of the expensive parts

A developer working intensively with AI reports that their cognitive load goes up, not down. Four mechanisms, named from the inside — and each one turns out to have a literature behind it.

Mechanism 01 — Pace

The rate of development rises. The machine sets a tempo that does not tire, and the day is paced by something with no need of recovery.

throughput ↑ · instability ↑ (DORA)

Mechanism 02 — Residue

Only the difficult decisions still reach the human. The easy, low-load work that used to pace a day is the first thing automated away.

the left-over principle

Mechanism 03 — Verification

Proof-reading thought-through matter. Fluent, confident, occasionally wrong output has no cheap rejection signal — you must reconstruct the reasoning to know whether to trust it.

the verification tax

Mechanism 04 — Fragmentation

Running several agents at once is the efficient move, so context switching becomes constant, and several unfinished problems sit in mind at the same time.

attention residue · 2–4 agent ceiling

03 Claim

The mechanism

Jevons's paradox, one level up

The essay already relies on Jevons to argue that cheap code expands the demand for software rather than shrinking it. The same paradox runs at the level of the person — and this is the part the optimistic version left out.

1865 · COAL

Engines get more efficient
→ each lump of coal does more work
→ power gets cheaper
→ a thousand new uses appear
→ total consumption soars

Britain did not end up with spare coal. Efficiency consumed the savings and asked for more.

2026 · ATTENTION

Code gets cheaper to produce
→ each hour of yours does more work
→ supervision gets cheaper per unit
→ more work runs in parallel
→ total attention spent soars

The developer does not end up with spare attention. Every hour the machine gives back is reinvested in supervising more work in flight, and the surplus is never banked.

Automation takes the easy parts of a task and leaves the human the hard residue — then asks them to stay alert for it indefinitely. Lisanne Bainbridge · Ironies of Automation · 1983

This is why the phenomenon is not new and not a complaint. It is a documented structural pattern — described forty years before these tools existed, in aviation and process control — arriving in a new domain.

04 Payoff

The consequence

Human attention is the binding constraint

Not compute. Not model capability. The ceiling on AI-augmented output is how much of it one accountable person can hold in mind and vouch for — and unlike compute, you cannot buy more of it.

Which inverts a good deal of strategy. Buying capability past the point where anyone can supervise it does not produce more usable output. It produces more unverified output, which is a liability rather than an asset.

It cuts against the usual worry, and both things are true. The standard fear is that AI makes people mentally lazy — judgment withering from disuse. The reported experience of the people using it hardest is the opposite: a denser, heavier day, because what was automated away first is the low-load work that used to pace it. Two different failure modes, landing on two different groups. The honest version of the argument says both.
