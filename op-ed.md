# The Machines Will Be Smarter. The Question Is Whether We'll Be Wise.

We have started saying it almost casually: artificial intelligence is becoming more intelligent
than we are. In many fields it already is. But that sentence hides three very different claims, and
nearly every muddled argument about our future comes from sliding between them.

The first is modest and plainly true: machines already beat us at particular tasks, from chess to
reading a medical scan. We made our peace with that long ago — no one feels diminished by a
calculator. The third is the science-fiction one: a superintelligence that someday outstrips all of
us at everything. It may come; reasonable people disagree. But almost all the public noise is
conducted in that key, as if the only question worth asking were the one we can't yet answer.

The claim that actually matters is the middle one, and it is arriving now: machines that match or
beat the typical person across most of the cognitive work our economy and institutions run on. For
the first time, the kind of thinking we treated as scarce, valuable, and distinctively human is
becoming abundant and cheap. We don't need to settle whether a god is coming to see that the ground
has already shifted under our feet.

Here is the shift, in one idea. For all of history we bundled together three things that are not the
same: **capability**, what can be done; **value**, what the market will pay for; and **worth**, what
makes a life matter. They rode together so closely we forgot they were different. The skilled doctor
knew things others didn't, was paid well for it, and drew dignity from the knowing — knowledge,
reward, and standing in a single package. Cheap, abundant machine intelligence is pulling that
package apart. Capability is migrating to machines. Value is being repriced in its wake. And worth
was never really the same thing as either — we only let ourselves believe it was.

Almost everything that frightens us about AI is a seam coming open between those three. Take the
economy. Markets reward what is scarce. For two centuries, skilled thinking paid well because it was
scarce — only so many people could analyze, diagnose, write, or design. Remove that bottleneck and
the price of cognitive work drifts toward the cost of the electricity that produces it, even as the
sheer quantity of valuable output explodes. More gets done; less of it flows to anyone as a wage.
The gains go instead to whoever owns what stays scarce: the computing power, the energy, the data,
the trusted brands, and the physical capacity to act in the world. The real divide of the AI economy
isn't humans versus machines. It's between those who own the scarce things and those who used to
sell their thinking. That is a question of distribution, and it is political to the core.

Which means the most anxious question — what work is left for people? — is usually asked the wrong
way. "What can humans still do that machines can't?" sets us running a race on the machine's track,
retreating a little further every year. Better to ask two different questions. What will the world
still *demand* of people? Probably work fused to the messy physical world (the trades, care), work
where a human must be accountable (someone who can be blamed, sued, thanked — a machine cannot),
and work that sits at the interface, directing the machines and judging their output. And what do we
*want* to keep human, whether or not a machine could do it cheaper? Caring for one another, raising
children, making and stewarding, governing ourselves — citizenship can't be automated without
ceasing to be self-rule.

But it would be dishonest to let those tidy lists stand, because between today's world and that one
lies a transition, and transitions like this have been brutal and unfair to the people caught in
them — even when the long-run average came out ahead. The weavers displaced by the power loom were
part of a process that eventually lifted everyone's living standards. It was no comfort to the
weavers, who lived and died inside the displacement and never saw the better world their suffering
helped buy. To tell a fifty-year-old whose profession just dissolved to "focus on the human work" is,
said carelessly, a kind of cruelty. The average can improve while millions of particular lives break.
Whether this is remembered as a liberation or a betrayal will turn far less on how clever the
machines get than on whether we share the gains and cushion the fall.

So is it even worth striving to be intelligent anymore, if a machine out-thinks you for the price of
some electricity? Yes — but a different intelligence than we've prized. We don't lift weights because
the world is short of forklifts; we lift for what it does to the lifter. Thinking is the same. The
struggle to work something out for yourself is how you build judgment and become a self, not just an
inefficient way to get an answer the machine could hand you. So stop racing the machine on raw recall
and calculation; you'll lose, and the losing teaches nothing. Cultivate instead what the machine
doesn't have: wisdom — knowing which things are worth doing; discernment — telling the true from the
merely fluent in a world flooded with persuasive synthetic speech; and the autonomy to use the
machine's answers without surrendering the judgment that decides what to do with them. This is not a
hobby. A people that gratefully stops thinking because a machine will think for it doesn't escape a
chore; it becomes easy to steer by whoever owns the machine. Independent thought is becoming a civic
duty, the inner equivalent of a free press.

And that points to the question hiding under all the others: not how intelligent the machines become,
but who controls them. Today, superhuman capability is produced by a handful of organizations
commanding rare concentrations of compute and capital. This is why a technical-sounding choice —
whether the most capable models are released openly, for anyone to run and inspect, or kept locked
behind a company's doors and rented out — is really a choice about power and freedom. Open models are
the main counterweight to a world where a few firms own the means of thought: they let a hospital, a
town, or a nation run their own intelligence instead of renting it on terms someone else sets. They
also, irreversibly, put capability into the hands of people who would misuse it, and a model released
cannot be recalled. That dilemma is real and unresolved, and anyone who tells you it's easy — in
either direction — is selling something. But notice what open models decide: not how high the
frontier reaches, but how widely the reach is shared. They set the floor of who gets left behind.

Put it all together and the lesson is the opposite of helplessness. The technology doesn't decide the
outcome. The same capability can be built into a liberation or a cage, and which we get depends on
choices still open to us: whether the gains are shared or hoarded, whether the displaced are carried
or abandoned, whether capability is spread or concentrated, whether a freed humanity invests in
meaning or drifts in comfortable purposelessness. The hardest problems of this age are not technical
but human — problems of distribution, power, and meaning — and those are exactly the problems no
machine, however intelligent, can solve for us, because they are about what *we* want.

The weaver couldn't choose whether the loom would come. The society around him could have chosen how
to meet it, and largely chose not to. We are at our own version of that threshold, with more warning
and more power to act. The machines will keep getting smarter. The only question that was ever really
ours is whether we'll be wise.
