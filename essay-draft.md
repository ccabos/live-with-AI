# Living With AI That Is More Intelligent Than Us

*A philosophical essay. Draft — opening movement (introduction, thesis, and the
question of what "intelligence" means).*

---

## I. The sentence we keep saying

We have started saying, almost casually, that artificial intelligence is becoming more
intelligent than we are. In some rooms the sentence is spoken with awe, in others with
dread, but it is increasingly spoken as though its meaning were obvious. It is not. The
sentence hides at least three different claims, and almost every confused argument about
our future comes from sliding silently between them.

The first claim is the modest one, and it is simply true: machines already surpass us at
particular tasks. They play better chess, fold proteins we could not fold, read a
medical scan more reliably than a tired clinician, and produce fluent prose in a hundred
languages at once. This kind of narrow superiority is not new — a pocket calculator has
out-computed every human alive since the 1970s — and we long ago made our peace with it.
No one feels diminished by a calculator.

The second claim is larger and is the one now genuinely arriving: that machines can
match or beat the typical human across most of the cognitive work that our economies and
institutions actually run on. Not one game, but the general capacity to read, reason,
write, summarize, plan, and explain. This is the regime we are entering, and it is
unsettling precisely because it is not narrow. It touches the kind of thinking that most
people are paid for, and that many people quietly use to know who they are.

The third claim is the speculative one, the one that fills the films: a superintelligence
that exceeds not just the average human but the best of us, and the whole of us together,
including at the task of improving itself. It may come; it may not; reasonable people who
have looked closely disagree. But it is worth noticing how much of the public conversation
is conducted entirely in the key of this third claim, as though the only question worth
asking were the science-fiction one.

I want to argue that the urgent question lives in the second regime, not the third. We do
not need to settle whether a god is coming to recognize that something has already changed
beneath our feet: for the first time, the kind of cognitive ability that our civilization
treated as scarce, valuable, and distinctively human is becoming abundant and cheap. That
fact alone is enough to unsettle how we live together, how we measure worth, and what we
tell our children to become. We can leave the gods to the theologians and still have more
than enough to think about.

## II. Three things we have always bundled together

Here is the central move of this essay, the one everything else will hang on. When we
worry that AI is becoming more intelligent than us, we are bundling together three things
that a clear mind should hold apart. Call them **capability**, **value**, and **worth**.

*Capability* is what can be done — the power to solve a problem, produce an output, get a
result. *Value*, in the sense our economies use the word, is what we are willing to reward;
it is capability priced by scarcity and demand. *Worth* is something else entirely: it is
what makes a life matter, the standing a person has simply as a person, the meaning we find
in an act regardless of what it produces or what it fetches in a market.

For most of human history these three rode together so closely that we never had to
separate them. The capable person was valuable, and the valuable person was accorded worth.
The skilled physician knew things others did not (capability), was paid and esteemed for it
(value), and drew dignity from the knowing (worth). Knowledge, reward, and standing arrived
in a single bundle, and our institutions — our schools, our labor markets, our very notions
of merit — were built on the assumption that the bundle would hold.

The arrival of cheap, abundant, superhuman cognition is the event that pulls the bundle
apart. Capability is migrating, rapidly and probably irreversibly, into machines. Value —
economic value — is being repriced in its wake, and not in ways that track our intuitions
about who deserves what. And worth, I will argue, was never really the same thing as either
of the other two; we only let ourselves believe it was because the bundle held for so long.

So the thesis of this essay is not a prediction about how intelligent machines will become.
It is a claim about what we must learn to think clearly: **that capability, value, and worth
are coming apart, and that the central task of a society living with superhuman AI is to
decide, deliberately, how to hold them once they no longer hold themselves.** Almost every
question that follows — how we live together, how the economy changes, which work to do,
whether to keep striving to think — is a question about the seams between these three. I
will not arrive at a single verdict about whether the future is bright or dark, because I do
not believe the technology decides that. What I will argue is that the outcome turns on
choices that are still ours to make, and that thinking clearly about these three words is
the precondition for making them well.

## III. What we mean, and do not mean, by "intelligence"

Before we can say that a machine has more intelligence than a human, we owe ourselves an
answer to a question we usually skip: more of *what*? "Intelligence" is not one thing. It is
a loose bag of quite different capacities that happen to correlate in human beings, and the
correlation has misled us into thinking they are a single substance that one could simply
have more or less of.

Consider how many different things the one word carries. There is the intelligence of
*knowledge* — the recall and synthesis of what is already known — at which machines are now
flatly superhuman, having read more than any person could read in a thousand lifetimes.
There is *fluid reasoning*, the ability to work out something genuinely new in the moment;
here machines are rising fast and unevenly, sometimes startling us, sometimes failing at
what a child finds easy. There is *embodied* intelligence, the competence of a body moving
through an unstructured physical world — the plumber's hands, the nurse's gentleness, the
improvisation of a cook — where machines remain clumsy and expensive. There is *social* and
*relational* intelligence, the reading of a room, the building of trust, the simple fact of
being *with* another person; machines can now mimic its surface convincingly while holding
none of it. And there is the intelligence we are least able to automate because it may not
be the kind of thing a tool can have at all: *moral* intelligence, the weighing of what
ought to matter, and *wisdom*, the judgment that knows what is worth doing and is willing to
stand behind the consequences.

Notice that machines are colonizing the top of this list and have barely touched the
bottom. They are becoming vast reservoirs of knowledge and increasingly able reasoners while
remaining strangers to presence, responsibility, and care. This is why the flat sentence —
"AI is more intelligent than humans" — is a kind of category error. It treats a tall stack
of distinct capacities as a single height to be measured. On some of those capacities the
machine now towers over us. On others it is not even in the room, and on a few it is not
clear that it can be, because they require a subject who has something at stake, who can be
held to account, who can suffer the consequences of being wrong.

This distinction is not a comfort blanket, a way of reassuring ourselves that we will always
have a corner the machines cannot reach. Some of those corners will shrink; the embodied and
even the social ones may erode faster than we expect. It is rather an analytic tool, and we
will need it at every step. When we ask which work humans should do, we will be asking which
kinds of intelligence remain genuinely ours or genuinely demanded. When we ask whether it is
still worth striving to be intelligent, we will find that the answer depends entirely on
*which* intelligence we mean — that to chase the machine on raw capability is folly, while to
cultivate judgment, autonomy, and wisdom may be more necessary than it has ever been. And
when we ask how to live together, we will see that the capacities machines cannot hold —
trust, accountability, the authority to decide — are precisely the ones our common life will
have to be rebuilt around.

That rebuilding is the subject of what follows. We begin where the pressure is already being
felt: in the principles by which we live together, and in an economy that is quietly learning
to measure value by a different ruler.
